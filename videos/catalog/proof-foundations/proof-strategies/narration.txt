A few odd-number sums suggest a square pattern, but examples cannot establish infinitely many cases. Induction separates the job into a starting case and a rule that carries an arbitrary successful case to the next.

For the sum of the first n positive odd integers, the first case is n equals one. Both sides equal one. This supplies an actual starting point; the later implication alone would not guarantee any case begins.

Assume the first n odd integers sum to n squared. The next odd integer is two n plus one. Adding it gives n squared plus two n plus one, exactly the expansion of the next square.

Because n was arbitrary, the step applies repeatedly from the verified first case. The picture of sixteen squares illustrates n equals four; the algebraic implication proves the entire chain for every positive integer n.
