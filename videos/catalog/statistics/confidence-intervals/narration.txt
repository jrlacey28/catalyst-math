A confidence interval adds an uncertainty range to an estimate. Assume twenty-five independent observations from a normal population with known standard deviation two, and a sample mean of ten. These assumptions determine the interval procedure we are about to use.

The mean's standard error is two divided by five, or zero point four. Multiplying by the normal cutoff, approximately one point nine six, gives a margin of zero point seven eight four. Add and subtract that margin to find the endpoints.

The ninety-five-percent confidence level describes repeated use of the method. Across repeated samples under these assumptions, about ninety-five percent of the resulting intervals cover the fixed population mean. It does not say that ninety-five percent of individual observations lie in this interval.

With the same known variability and confidence level, quadrupling sample size halves the margin. If the population standard deviation is estimated instead, use an appropriate method such as a t interval under its assumptions; do not automatically reuse the known-sigma formula.
