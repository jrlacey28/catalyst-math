A variable names a quantity so we can describe a relationship without choosing its value immediately. If each notebook costs three dollars, n can count notebooks and c can describe the corresponding cost in dollars.

Once an input is supplied, substitution makes the rule concrete. Four notebooks means replace n with four everywhere it appears. Three times four gives twelve dollars; the letter was a placeholder for the quantity, not an extra operation.

Sometimes the output is known instead. A bill of twenty one dollars creates an equation whose unknown is the notebook count. Seven groups of three fill that total, so solving recovers seven notebooks.

The situation restricts which values make sense. A notebook count uses nonnegative whole numbers, even though the formula can be evaluated at other real inputs. Naming a variable should include its meaning, units, and allowed values.
