A linear inequality can represent a limit on resources. If each item costs three dollars plus a fixed two-dollar charge, staying within seventeen dollars requires three x plus two at most seventeen. Solving gives at most five items.

Now add another requirement: buy at least two items. This produces a second interval, extending rightward from two. Meeting one requirement is not sufficient; a valid purchase must satisfy the budget and the minimum quantity together.

The overlap of the two solution sets runs from two through five, including both endpoints. This compound inequality is an intersection: every value inside meets both restrictions, while a value outside fails at least one of them.

Finally restore the context of whole items. Three and a half lies inside the real interval but is not a permitted whole-item count. The feasible answers are two, three, four, and five, after combining algebraic and contextual restrictions.
