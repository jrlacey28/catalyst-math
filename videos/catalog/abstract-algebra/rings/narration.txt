A ring supports addition, subtraction, and multiplication with distributive laws. The integers are a familiar example. Multiplication need not supply inverses for every nonzero element: dividing one by two leaves the integer system altogether.

Now calculate with remainders modulo six. Addition and multiplication still make a ring, but two times three becomes zero. Both factors were nonzero classes. Such factors are called zero divisors, and they prevent familiar cancellation arguments.

For example, twice one and twice four both leave remainder two. Cancelling the common two would falsely say one equals four. Division by a factor requires an appropriate inverse or another justified cancellation property, not merely matching notation.

The multiples of two modulo six are zero, two, and four, so two has no multiplicative inverse. A field is a nontrivial commutative ring with identity in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse.
