Quadrature replaces an integral with a computable weighted sum. For the square function from zero to two, the exact area is eight thirds. We can use that known value to examine how a numerical approximation improves with refinement.

One trapezoid joins the endpoint heights by a straight line. Its area is interval width times the average height, giving four. The chord lies above this convex graph, so the estimate is too large for a geometrically understandable reason.

Split the interval into two equal pieces. The smaller trapezoids give one half plus two and a half, totaling three. The approximation is closer, and we can measure the remaining error rather than assuming more pieces automatically make it negligible.

For this quadratic and equal subdivisions, direct summation gives the exact error four divided by three n squared. Doubling the number of pieces quarters the error. General error bounds require assumptions such as bounded derivatives; this exact formula is specific to our example.
