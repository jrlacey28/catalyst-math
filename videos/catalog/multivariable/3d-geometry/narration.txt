Three-dimensional coordinates locate a point using three perpendicular directions. A surface equation restricts which triples are allowed. On the plane z equals x plus y, the first two coordinates determine the third; one and one therefore require height two.

Distance extends the Pythagorean theorem. From the origin to one, one, two, the squared contributions are one, one, and four. Their sum is six, so the straight-line distance is square root of six, not the sum of the components.

Rearranging the plane equation reveals a normal vector: negative one, negative one, one. Every displacement lying in the plane has dot product zero with this normal. The normal encodes orientation rather than a particular point on the plane.

A slice helps us understand a curved surface. Hold y equal to one on the paraboloid, and the remaining cross-section is z equals x squared plus one. Fixing a coordinate reduces a three-dimensional question to a familiar two-dimensional graph.
