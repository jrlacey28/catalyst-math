The greatest common divisor is the largest positive integer dividing both inputs. The Euclidean algorithm repeatedly replaces a pair by the smaller number and the remainder. Subtracting a multiple preserves exactly the common divisors.

After dividing eighty-four by thirty, the remainder is twenty-four. Dividing thirty by twenty-four leaves six; dividing twenty-four by six leaves zero. The last nonzero remainder is six, so the greatest common divisor is six.

Reverse the substitutions to express the divisor as an integer combination of the original numbers. Six is thirty minus twenty-four, and twenty-four is eighty-four minus twice thirty. Combining these expressions gives three times thirty minus eighty-four.

Why does the combination matter? Every common divisor must divide every integer combination, including six. Since six itself divides both numbers, it is greatest. This is a proof of the answer, not merely an observed remainder pattern.
