An eigenvector is a nonzero vector whose image is a scalar multiple of itself. For this matrix, one, one becomes three, three. The arrow grows but stays on the same line, so it is an eigenvector with eigenvalue three.

To find every eigenvector for eigenvalue three, solve the homogeneous system formed by A minus three times the identity. Both equations reduce to y equals x. Therefore every nonzero multiple of one, one belongs to this eigendirection.

There is another eigendirection: one, negative one remains unchanged, giving eigenvalue one. Two independent eigenvectors form a useful coordinate system here. Along these directions the same transformation acts by separate scalar multipliers rather than mixed coordinate formulas.

Decompose two, zero into one copy of each eigendirection. Linearity lets us transform those pieces separately, then recombine them. The result is four, two. This simplification explains why an eigenvector basis is powerful when such a basis exists.
