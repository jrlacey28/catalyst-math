An algorithm is a precise sequence of steps for solving a class of problems. To find ten in this sorted list, binary search compares with a middle value instead of inspecting every entry from the beginning.

Comparing ten with the lower middle value eight eliminates every entry up to eight. Sorted order justifies the elimination: none of those smaller values can be the target. The remaining candidates are ten, twelve, fourteen, and sixteen.

Comparing with twelve narrows the search again, leaving ten as the only candidate on its left. Each step preserves the invariant that a present target remains inside the candidate range. Shrinking that range ensures the procedure eventually terminates.

Repeated halving explains the logarithmic growth in comparisons. This efficiency depends on ordered data and a method for accessing middle entries. Without sorted order, discarding half could throw away the target, so speed cannot replace the algorithm's assumptions.
