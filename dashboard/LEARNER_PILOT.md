# A small, honest learner pilot

The purpose is to find barriers and examine independent learning, not to claim that Catalyst is the fastest way to learn. Automated checks verify software and selected mathematical results; they are not observations of human learning. No participant outcomes have been collected for this release.

## Participation and privacy

Use consenting adult volunteers for the initial pilot. Explain the tasks, approximate duration, optional delayed return, what will be recorded, and that stopping has no penalty. A different process with appropriate guardian consent and privacy review is needed before involving children. Participation is separate from assessment or course access.

Create a separate local learning space with a pseudonym. The app's About → Learner pilot page saves only observations entered after the learner explicitly chooses to participate. It uploads nothing and does not change stars. Stopping disables further observation entry. Existing notes remain in that learner's progress download. Share an export only if the learner deliberately agrees; inspect it first because it also contains their homework. Do not publish personal exports in the source repository. Local profiles are not password-protected accounts.

## First round: usability

Recruit a small, varied group, for example 5–8 volunteers with different math experience, devices and access needs. This is a practical initial sample, not a statistically powered efficacy trial. Ask each person to work naturally; record observations rather than rescuing immediately.

1. Find an appropriate starting point and open a lesson.
2. Attempt three problems, explain one answer, and locate calculator help if needed.
3. Enter two actual written steps, inspect the feedback, and revise a questionable step.
4. Open a foundation repair and return to the original draft.
5. Finish a partially worked example, then attempt a fresh problem without help.
6. Change a connected project model, save a decision, leave and return.
7. Find the skill tree and explain what a star does and does not mean.

Capture task completion, time spent searching, navigation mistakes, lost work, unclear feedback, help requested, and the learner's own explanation of what happened. Test keyboard-only use, a narrow screen and reduced motion where relevant. Ask “What did you expect here?” rather than suggesting the desired response. Fix repeated barriers before expanding recruitment.

## Second round: learning and retention

Choose one small concept per participant. Have a qualified reviewer prepare equivalent but different forms before the session: baseline, immediate unaided check, next-day recall and approximately one-week recall. Each form should include a routine calculation, a reason or error-analysis task, and unfamiliar transfer. Match the mathematical demand and conditions without reusing values, diagrams or answer patterns. Do not show later forms during instruction. For proof topics, include proof and counterexample tasks reviewed by a human; multiple-choice success is insufficient.

Record the baseline, then let the learner use the normal lesson cycle. Record time and assistance during instruction. Give the immediate check without video, examples, calculator guidance or AI help. A request for substantive help turns that attempt into practice; retain that record and use a fresh form for independent evidence. Invite, rather than silently schedule, the delayed returns. Record actual dates and any intervening study. Do not count missed returns as wrong answers.

Use a prewritten rubric: correct result; valid transformations and domain conditions; explanation of why a method applies; ability to choose a method in an unfamiliar context. Report scores by component alongside time, hints, confidence and completion. Include errors and incomplete attempts. Compare immediate performance with delayed performance; do not infer retention from a same-day pass or a simulated software date.

For a later comparison study, predefine the question, primary outcome and analysis, estimate the sample size with suitable expertise, and randomly assign or counterbalance comparable lesson formats. A small uncontrolled pilot can reveal design problems and promising patterns; it cannot establish causal superiority or universal learning speed.

## Observation sheet

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| Pseudonym, device, access needs | Voluntary, minimum necessary |
| Topic and baseline task/form | Include prerequisites and rubric |
| Learning time and assistance | Actual time, help type, interruptions |
| Where the learner hesitated | Observed action and their words |
| Immediate independent evidence | Calculation, reasoning, transfer |
| Delayed evidence | Actual date, new form, intervening study |
| Change to make | Specific usability or content finding |

Publish only consented, anonymized aggregates with limitations, not raw learner records. Preserve a distinction between observed findings and hypotheses.

## Design references

The pilot examines the use of spaced retrieval, interleaved worked examples and problems, connections between visual and symbolic representations, and explanatory questions discussed in the [IES organizing-instruction practice guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1). The [IES algebra practice guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/20) informs work on structure, analyzing solved examples and choosing methods. These support design choices; they do not validate this particular implementation.

Assisted performance and unaided learning must be measured separately. A [2025 field study of generative-AI tutoring](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/) illustrates why success while using help should not be treated as evidence of later independent skill. Catalyst's local AI is optional help, not a grader.
